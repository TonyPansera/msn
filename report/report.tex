\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We wrote a Metropolis Monte Carlo code that exchanges Cu and Zn atoms on a fixed body-centred cubic lattice of 432 sites, with a pair energy model limited to first and second neighbours.
The code reproduces the exact values of the perfect B2 structure (cohesive energy \SI{2.484}{eV/atom}) and of a random alloy.
Scanning the temperature from \SIrange{200}{1600}{K}, we follow the energy, the heat capacity, the long-range order parameter $\eta$, its susceptibility and the Cowley short-range order parameters.
The three estimates of the transition temperature all give $\Tc \approx \SI{720}{K}$, close to the experimental value of about \SI{740}{K}.
Short-range order survives above $\Tc$.
\end{abstract}

% =====================================================================
\section{Introduction}

$\beta$-brass is the CuZn alloy with about 50\,\% Zn.
At low temperature it is ordered in the B2 (CsCl-type) structure: the atoms sit on a body-centred cubic (bcc) lattice, Cu on the cube corners and Zn on the cube centres.
When the temperature rises, Cu and Zn atoms swap places more and more often.
Around \SI{740}{K} the distinction between the two kinds of sites disappears: the alloy becomes a disordered bcc solid solution.
This is a classic order--disorder transition, first described by Bragg and Williams with a mean-field theory.

In this project the atoms do not move away from the lattice sites (\enquote{rigid lattice}): only the occupation of each site by Cu or Zn changes.
The energy is a sum of pair interactions between first and second neighbours.
We sample the equilibrium configurations with the Metropolis algorithm and measure how the order is lost when the temperature increases.

\section{Model and method}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Lattice and neighbours}

At low temperature CuZn has the ordered B2 structure (CsCl type), made of two interpenetrating simple cubic sublattices $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
The cubic cell of parameter $a$ contains two sites (\cref{fig:cell}): the $\alpha$ site at the corner $(0,0,0)$, occupied by Cu in B2, and the $\beta$ site at the centre $(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12)$, occupied by Zn.
Whatever the species on them, the sites form a bcc lattice, which stays fixed during the simulation.
The simulation box contains $6\times6\times6$ cells, that is $N = 2\times 6^3 = 432$ sites (216 Cu and 216 Zn, $c_\Zn=1/2$), with periodic boundary conditions in the three directions.
The reference configuration at $T=\SI{0}{K}$ is the B2 cell repeated $6\times6\times6$ times.

Each site has two neighbour shells (\cref{tab:parameters}): 8 first neighbours at distance $\sqrt{3}a/2$, all on the other sublattice, and 6 second neighbours at distance $a$, on the same sublattice.
The list of these 14 neighbours is built once for every site at the start of the program, applying the periodic boundary conditions (modulo 6 on each cell coordinate).
This table never changes: only the occupation of the sites does.

\subsection{Energy}

The energy of a configuration is
\begin{equation}
  E = \sum_{\langle i,j\rangle_1} J_{\sigma_i\sigma_j} + \sum_{\langle i,j\rangle_2} I_{\sigma_i\sigma_j},
  \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_i\in\{\Cu,\Zn\}$ is the atom on site $i$, and the sums run over first- and second-neighbour pairs, each pair counted once.
The pair energies are given in \cref{tab:parameters}.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \caption{Neighbour shells and pair energies of the model in eV. $J$ acts between first neighbours, $I$ between second neighbours. The last column is the \enquote{ordering energy} $w = X_{\Cu\Cu}+X_{\Zn\Zn}-2X_{\Cu\Zn}$ of each shell ($X=J$ or $I$): $w>0$ favours unlike pairs, $w<0$ favours like pairs.}
  \label{tab:parameters}
  \begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
    \toprule
    Shell & Distance & Neighbours & Cu--Cu & Zn--Zn & Cu--Zn & $w$ \\
    \midrule
    1st neighbours ($J$) & $\sqrt{3}a/2$ & 8 & $-0.873$ & $-0.338$ & $-0.621$ & $+0.031$ \\
    2nd neighbours ($I$) & $a$ & 6 & $\phantom{-}0.000$ & $\phantom{-}0.000$ & $+0.005$ & $-0.010$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The first shell favours Cu--Zn pairs ($w_1>0$) and the second shell favours pairs of the same species ($w_2<0$).
In the B2 structure the 8 first neighbours of every atom are of the other species and the 6 second neighbours of the same species, so both shells push towards B2 order.

\subsection{Monte Carlo simulation}

We sample the Boltzmann distribution $P\propto e^{-E/k_BT}$ with the Metropolis algorithm~\cite{HayounDammak2026}, whose main lines are given in \cref{alg:metropolis}.
Starting from the perfect B2 structure, each step draws a random atom $i$ and one of its 8 first neighbours $j$, computes the energy $E_\mathrm{after}$ after exchanging them, and accepts the exchange with probability $\min\left(1,e^{-(E_\mathrm{after}-E)/k_BT}\right)$; otherwise the configuration is kept.
One \emph{sweep} is $N=432$ steps, so that every atom is drawn once on average (this is purely our choice to use this number for one sweep, it has no theorical meaning. We explain why in part \ref{sec:measured}).
The first $n_\mathrm{eq}$ steps are discarded (\cref{sec:equilibration}), then the quantities of \cref{sec:measured} are measured every $m$ steps.

\begin{algorithm}[ht!]
\caption{Metropolis. The black lines are common to both versions of the run: the blue lines are the measurement run (quantities recorded every $m$ steps after equilibration), the orange line is the equilibration study (energy recorded after every step).}
\label{alg:metropolis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\Require $T$, $n_{\mathrm{eq}}$, \A{$n_{\mathrm{after}}$, $m$ (\texttt{measure\_every})}

\State Initialize the system at $0K$
\State Compute the initial total energy $E$
\State Initialize empty lists $energies$\A{, $\eta,\alpha_1,\alpha_2$}

\For{$k=0$ to $n_{\mathrm{eq}}$\A{$+\,n_{\mathrm{after}}$}$-1$}

    \State Randomly select an atom $i$
    \State Randomly select one of its 8 neighbours $j$
    \State Compute the energy after the swap $E_{\mathrm{after}}$
    \State Calculate the acceptance probability:
    $P \gets \min\left(1,e^{-(E_{\mathrm{after}}-E)/(k_BT)}\right)$

    \If{the swap is accepted with probability $P$}
        \State Perform the swap between $i$ and $j$
        \State $E \gets E_{\mathrm{after}}$
    \EndIf

    \State \B{Append $E$ to $energies$}

    \IfA{$k \geq n_{\mathrm{eq}}$ \textbf{and} $(k-n_{\mathrm{eq}}) \bmod m = 0$}
        \State \A{Compute $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ (Cowley) and $|\eta|$}
        \State \A{Append $E$ to $energies$}
        \State \A{Append $|\eta|,\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ to their respective lists}
    \EndIfA

\EndFor

\State \Return $energies$\A{, $\eta,\alpha_1,\alpha_2$}

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Measured quantities}
\label{sec:measured}

After equilibration, we record once per sweep the energy $E$, the long-range order parameter and the two Cowley parameters. We chose to record those values every sweep and not every step, as one step might do nothing and it is better to let the system evolve a little bit before sampling any value. This is purely our choice.
\begin{equation}
  \eta = \frac{N_\Cu^{(\alpha)}-N_\Cu^{(\beta)}}{N_\Cu^{(\alpha)}+N_\Cu^{(\beta)}}, \qquad
  \alpha_n = 1-\frac{p_n^{\Zn|\Cu}}{c_\Zn}\quad (n=1,2),
\end{equation}
Here $N_\Cu^{(\alpha)}$ is the number of Cu atoms on sublattice $\alpha$, $p_n^{\Zn|\Cu}$ the probability that a neighbour of shell $n$ of a Cu atom is a Zn atom, and $c_\Zn=1/2$.
The perfect B2 order gives $\eta=1$, $\alpha_1=-1$ and $\alpha_2=+1$; a random alloy gives $\eta=\alpha_1=\alpha_2=0$.
In a finite box the ordered state can also form with Cu on $\beta$ ($\eta<0$), and near $\Tc$ the system goes back and forth between the two.
We therefore average $|\eta|$, which measures the amount of order whatever its sign.

From the series of measurements we compute the means $\langle E\rangle$, $\langle|\eta|\rangle$, $\langle\alpha_n\rangle$, and two response functions from the fluctuations:
\begin{equation}
  C_v = \frac{\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2}{k_BT^2},\qquad
  \chi = \frac{N\left(\langle\eta^2\rangle-\langle|\eta|\rangle^2\right)}{k_BT}.
  \label{eq:fluctuations}
\end{equation}
$C_v$ is the heat capacity of the whole box (in \si{eV/K}) and $\chi$ the susceptibility of the order parameter (in \si{eV^{-1}}).

\section{Equilibration}
\label{sec:equilibration}

Every run starts from the perfect B2 structure, which is not representative of the equilibrium.
The first steps must be discarded until the system has \enquote{forgotten} this initial state.
To choose this number of steps, we record the energy after every step during \num{200000} steps at each temperature of the scan (\cref{fig:equilibration}).
The equilibrium energy $E_\mathrm{eq}$ and the size $\sigma$ of its fluctuations are the mean and standard deviation over the second half of the run; equilibrium is reached at the first step where $E \ge E_\mathrm{eq}-\sigma$.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{equilibration_energy.png}
  \caption{Equilibration from the perfect B2 structure: energy per atom during the first 120 sweeps at four temperatures (one value every 50 steps). The dotted line is the B2 energy.}
  \label{fig:equilibration}
\end{figure}

At low temperature (200--300~K) the B2 structure is already the equilibrium state and almost no exchange is accepted.
At high temperature, disorder sets in within a few thousand steps.
The slowest relaxations are found below and near the transition, with at most \num{11806}~steps (27~sweeps) at \SI{730}{K}.
This estimate is itself noisy, because the energy fluctuates strongly near $\Tc$.
We therefore discard $N_\mathrm{eq}=\num{50000}$ steps at every temperature, more than four times the slowest relaxation.

\section{Physical quantities and transition temperature}
\label{sec:results}

At each temperature tested, we ran the Metropolis algorithm from the B2 structure until equilibrium ($N_\mathrm{eq}$ steps, \cref{sec:equilibration}).
Once equilibrium was reached, we continued the run for 4000 sweeps and calculated the physical quantities of \cref{sec:measured} from the recorded series.
All the values are listed in \cref{tab:full}.

\subsection{Evolution with temperature}

\Cref{fig:snapshots} shows what the configurations look like.
At \SI{300}{K} the layer is a perfect checkerboard of Cu and Zn.
Near $\Tc$ the order is only partial, and at \SI{1200}{K} the atoms are spread at random over the two sublattices.

\paragraph{Energy and heat capacity.}
The mean energy (\cref{fig:energy}, left) starts from the B2 value and rises steeply between 600 and \SI{800}{K}, where most of the disorder appears.
The heat capacity (\cref{fig:energy}, right) has a clear peak at \SI{720}{K}; its value at \SI{700}{K} is almost the same, so the top of the peak is between 700 and \SI{720}{K}.

\paragraph{Long-range order.}
$\langle|\eta|\rangle$ (\cref{fig:order}, left) decreases slowly up to about \SI{600}{K}, then drops fast between 650 and \SI{800}{K}, and finally approaches the value of a random lattice, about $0.04$.
This value is not zero because, with only 216 Cu atoms, chance alone puts a few more of them on one sublattice.
The susceptibility (\cref{fig:order}, right) has a sharp peak at \SI{720}{K}, almost equal to its value at \SI{730}{K}.

\Cref{fig:hist} shows the distribution of $|\eta|$.
Below $\Tc$ it is narrow around a large value; above $\Tc$ it is narrow close to zero.
Near $\Tc$ it is very broad: the system wanders between ordered and disordered states.

  \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Short-range order.}
\Cref{fig:cowley} shows the Cowley parameters.
Below $\Tc$ the Cowley parameters follow the curves of $\eta$: the long-range order explains almost all the local order.
Above $\Tc$, however, $\langle\eta^2\rangle$ is almost zero while $\alpha_1$ stays clearly negative: at \SI{800}{K}, $\langle\alpha_1\rangle = -0.20$ whereas $-\langle\eta^2\rangle = -0.05$, and at \SI{1600}{K} $\langle\alpha_1\rangle$ is still $-0.07$.
Cu atoms keep more Zn first neighbours than in a random alloy: this \emph{short-range order} survives the loss of long-range order and fades slowly with temperature, as observed experimentally in ordering alloys.

    The difference between symbols and dashed lines above \SI{750}{K} is the short-range order.}

\subsection{Transition temperature}
\label{sec:tc}

The energy, the order parameter and the two response functions all change abruptly around the same temperature: the alloy goes through a phase transition.
Each quantity shows it in its own way:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \emph{Order parameter.} $\eta$ distinguishes the two sublattices: it is non-zero in the ordered phase and zero in the disordered phase, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ become equivalent.
  Its fast drop is the loss of long-range order, which defines the transition.
  \item \emph{Heat capacity.} $C_v = \mathrm{d}\langle E\rangle/\mathrm{d}T$ measures how fast the energy rises with temperature.
  \item \emph{Susceptibility.} $\chi$ measures the fluctuations of $\eta$. Its peak shows that the order is the least stable at the transition, where the system wanders between ordered and disordered states (\cref{fig:hist}).
\end{itemize}

\Cref{tab:tc} gives the three estimates.
They fall on the same grid temperature, \SI{720}{K}.
The grid step is \SI{10}{K} there, but the peaks are flat at the top, between 700 and \SI{730}{K} (\cref{tab:full}): we retain $\Tc \approx \SI{720}{K}$.

\begin{table}[!ht]
  \centering
  \caption{Transition temperature. The Monte Carlo values are read on the temperature measured (step \SI{10}{K} between 700 and \SI{760}{K}).}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our estimate of $\Tc$ is close to the experimental value~\cite{Kuper1956}, which is quite good for a model with only six parameters and no adjustment.

\paragraph{Order of the transition.}
In a first-order transition the order parameter and the energy jump at $\Tc$ (latent heat), and the ordered and disordered phases coexist.
In a second-order transition the order parameter goes to zero continuously, while the heat capacity and the susceptibility diverge.
Our results look continuous: $\langle|\eta|\rangle$ decreases regularly through $\Tc$ (0.54 at \SI{700}{K}, 0.43 at \SI{720}{K}, 0.31 at \SI{740}{K}, \cref{tab:full}), the energy shows no visible jump, and at \SI{720}{K} the histogram of $|\eta|$ is a single broad hump, not two separated peaks (\cref{fig:hist}).
This favours a second-order transition, which is the usual description of $\beta$-brass~\cite{Kittel2005}.
Our box of 432 atoms is too small to be sure of this.
In a finite box there is no true singularity: a jump of $\eta$ is rounded into a steep but continuous drop, and a divergence is cut into a finite peak (here $C_v^\mathrm{max}\approx\SI{0.06}{eV/K}$ and $\chi^\mathrm{max}\approx\SI{210}{eV^{-1}}$), because near $\Tc$ the correlation length becomes larger than the box.

\section{Conclusion}

We wrote and tested a Metropolis Monte Carlo code for the order--disorder transition of CuZn on a rigid bcc lattice.
The tests on the perfect B2 structure give the expected values.
The cohesive energy of the B2 phase is \SI{2.484}{eV/atom}.
Starting from B2, the system needs at most about \num{12000} steps to reach equilibrium, so we discarded \num{50000} steps before measuring during \num{4000} sweeps.
The energy, the heat capacity, the long-range order parameter and its susceptibility all show the transition at $\Tc \approx \SI{720}{K}$, close to the experimental value (about \SI{740}{K}).
Above the transition, Cu and Zn still prefer to be first neighbours: short-range order survives the loss of long-range order.
The main limits of this study are the small size of the box and the simple pair model; larger boxes and a finite-size analysis would be the next step.

\section{Code and reproduction}
\label{app:code}

The model is written in Python in \texttt{src/model.py} (one module, about 190 lines):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item the array \texttt{sous\_reseau} holds the species of each site (0 = Cu, 1 = Zn); site $i$ of cell $(x,y,z)$ and sublattice $s$ has index $i = 2(x+6y+36z)+s$, so even sites are $\alpha$ and odd sites $\beta$;
  \item \texttt{FIRST\_NEIGHBOURS} and \texttt{SECOND\_NEIGHBOURS} are the neighbour tables, built once at import;
  \item \texttt{get\_full\_energy()} computes \cref{eq:energy}; \texttt{energy\_after\_flip(E, i, j)} returns the energy after exchanging $i$ and $j$ from the local bonds only, without changing the configuration; \texttt{flipping\_proba} and \texttt{flipping\_action} apply the Metropolis rule;
  \item \texttt{cowley()} and \texttt{order\_parameter()} return $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$ and $\eta$;
  \item \texttt{metropolis\_before\_eq(n, T)} returns the energy after each of $n$ steps from B2 (equilibration test); \texttt{metropolis(n\_eq, n\_meas, T, measure\_every)} returns the series of $E$, $|\eta|$, $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ measured after equilibration.
\end{itemize}
The analysis notebook \texttt{analysis.ipynb} uses these functions interactively.
All the figures and tables of this report are produced by \texttt{report/make\_figures.py}.

\end{verbatim}
